VOLUME sa-o1 | Green Lantern: The Silver Age Omnibus Vol. 1 | John Broome & Gil Kane | 1959–1965 | 38 issues
ABOUT Julius Schwartz had already brought back the Flash with a new man under an old name, and Showcase #22 does it again: Hal Jordan, a test pilot at Ferris Aircraft, pulled out of a flight simulator by a dying alien who needs someone "utterly without fear". Everything that makes Green Lantern a space book is invented in these first few years — the Guardians of Oa, the Corps, the oath, the yellow weakness, Sinestro in #7 and Star Sapphire in #16. John Broome writes almost all of it as clean science-fiction puzzle stories. The thing to look at is Gil Kane: his figures are lean and athletic and always mid-motion, and he is the artist who first understood that the ring meant drawing a fist coming straight out of the page at you.

VOLUME sa-o2 | Green Lantern: The Silver Age Omnibus Vol. 2 | John Broome, Gardner Fox & Gil Kane | 1965–1970 | 40 issues
ABOUT The back half of the original run. Green Lantern #40 is the one everybody means: Hal meets the Golden Age Green Lantern, Alan Scott, and the Guardians tell the story of Krona, the renegade who looked back at the creation of the universe and let evil into it — a single panel of a giant hand at the dawn of time that Geoff Johns would build a whole decade on forty years later. Gil Kane's drawing loosens and gets more ambitious as the sixties go on. By the end the book is running out of road; the sales are falling and #75 is the last issue before DC hands it to two people who will tear it up.
CHAPTERS Green Lantern (1960) #36–75
VOLUME hth-o1 | Green Lantern/Green Arrow: Hard Travelin' Heroes Omnibus | Dennis O'Neil, Neal Adams & Mike Grell | 1970–1979 | 77 issues
ABOUT Green Lantern #76: an old man asks Hal Jordan why he has helped the blue skins and the orange skins on other planets and never once the black skins on his own, and Hal has no answer. Dennis O'Neil and Neal Adams put Green Arrow in the book as a loudmouth leftist conscience and send the two of them across America in a pickup truck, and comics have been arguing about relevance ever since. #85–86 is the one with Speedy and the needle. #87 introduces John Stewart. Look at Adams: photographic faces, real light, figures that weigh something — nothing at DC had looked like it. The book was cancelled at #89 and survived as a back-up in The Flash, then came back in 1976 with Mike Grell, and this omnibus prints all of it, the one skip being #88.

VOLUME johns-o1 | Green Lantern by Geoff Johns Omnibus Vol. 1 | Geoff Johns, Ethan Van Sciver & Ivan Reis | 2004–2007 | 45 issues
ABOUT Twenty-five years jump between the last book and this one, and none of them has an omnibus: the rest of the 1960 run, Emerald Twilight, where Hal goes mad and kills the Corps, and Kyle Rayner's whole decade as the last Green Lantern. Rebirth exists to undo that, and its trick is to make the madness a monster — Parallax, the yellow fear entity — so Hal was possessed rather than broken. Ethan Van Sciver draws it in obsessive detail, every ring construct full of tiny machinery. Then Geoff Johns builds outward: the Sinestro Corps War is where the emotional spectrum stops being a weakness and becomes a cosmology, with Ivan Reis drawing armies of yellow and green across double-page spreads. It ends on a prophecy, and the prophecy is the next book.

VOLUME glc-o1 | Green Lantern Corps by Tomasi & Gleason Omnibus Vol. 1 | Peter J. Tomasi, Dave Gibbons & Patrick Gleason | 2005–2009 | 52 issues
ABOUT The other half of the Johns years, and the book that made the Corps feel like a place rather than a backdrop. Guy Gardner and Kyle Rayner run the recruits; Kilowog yells at them; Mogo is a planet and Salaak files the paperwork. Dave Gibbons co-writes Recharge and the first stretch, then Peter Tomasi takes over and writes the Corps like a war novel — who dies, who breaks, who gets home. Patrick Gleason's art is the thing to watch: he loves alien bodies, and the book is full of Lanterns who are crystals, insects or a single giant eye. The Sinestro Corps War chapters are the same ones Johns Vol. 1 prints, interleaved here as they were published.

VOLUME johns-o2 | Green Lantern by Geoff Johns Omnibus Vol. 2 | Geoff Johns, Ivan Reis & Doug Mahnke | 2008–2010 | 40 issues
ABOUT Johns fills in the spectrum one colour at a time: Secret Origin retells Showcase #22 for a modern reader, the Red Lanterns vomit rage-blood in Rage of the Red Lanterns, Larfleeze hoards everything in Agent Orange, and then all seven Corps are on the board when the dead start to rise. Blackest Night is the event the whole run was built toward, and it is a zombie story with a rule: the Black Lanterns can see your emotions, so the only defence is to feel nothing. Ivan Reis draws the core series as wall-to-wall spectacle, every hero who ever died back in black rings. Doug Mahnke takes over the monthly from #43 and draws it uglier and better.
CHAPTERS Green Lantern (2005) #26–52 ; Blackest Night (2009) #0–8 ; DC Universe (2008) #0 ; Untold Tales of Blackest Night (2010) #1 ; Blackest Night: Tales of the Corps (2009) #1–2
VOLUME bd-o1 | Brightest Day Omnibus | Geoff Johns, Peter J. Tomasi & Ivan Reis | 2010–2011 | 25 issues
ABOUT Blackest Night ends with twelve people brought back to life by the white light, and this is what the White Lantern wants with them. Be warned: Hal Jordan is barely in it. The leads are Deadman, Aquaman, Martian Manhunter, Firestorm, Hawkman and Hawkgirl, and the book is a year-long biweekly mystery about why they were chosen. It is on this shelf because it is the direct sequel to the Lantern event and because the White Lantern is the spectrum's last colour. Ivan Reis, Patrick Gleason, Ardian Syaf and Scott Clark split the art, which is why the book shifts look from issue to issue; the Aquaman chapters are the best of it.
CHAPTERS Brightest Day (2010) #0–24
VOLUME glc-o2 | Green Lantern Corps by Tomasi & Gleason Omnibus Vol. 2 | Peter J. Tomasi & Patrick Gleason | 2009–2013 | 54 issues
ABOUT Blackest Night from the trenches, with Gleason drawing the dead Lanterns of Oa rising out of the crypts — his last stretch on the book. Then Guy Gardner's own title: Emerald Warriors is Tomasi writing Guy as what he always was under the bluster, the Lantern who goes where the others will not, and it runs straight into the War of the Green Lanterns, interleaved here with the Johns chapters. Then the New 52 relaunch, drawn by Fernando Pasarin: Guy and John Stewart lead a Corps being hunted by the Guardians' Third Army, and an early arc turns on John killing a captured fellow Lantern rather than let him be tortured into talking. Tomasi leaves at #20, the same month Johns does.

VOLUME johns-o3 | Green Lantern by Geoff Johns Omnibus Vol. 3 | Geoff Johns & Doug Mahnke | 2010–2013 | 44 issues
ABOUT The end of nine years. The first third is the Brightest Day years and the War of the Green Lanterns, where Krona — the man from the 1965 Silver Age issue — finally gets his story. Then the New 52 relaunch does the bold thing: Sinestro wears a green ring again, and the best stretch of the run is Hal and Sinestro forced to work together, two men who know each other better than anyone. #0 introduces Simon Baz, a Lebanese-American from Dearborn who gets a ring in an interrogation room. Doug Mahnke draws it with heavy blacks and creased, sweating faces. #20 is Johns's last issue and it is extra-sized, with an epilogue that walks the cast into the future one at a time. It is widely held to be one of the best farewell issues any long run has had.

VOLUME red-o1 | Red Lanterns: The New 52 Omnibus | Peter Milligan, Charles Soule & Various | 2011–2015 | 61 issues
ABOUT Atrocitus's book, and the stranger end of the spectrum: a title about a corps whose members are powered by rage and mostly cannot speak. Peter Milligan writes the first stretch as a horror book about anger with nowhere to go. Then Charles Soule takes over and does the obvious, brilliant thing — he puts Guy Gardner in a red ring, undercover and then not, and the book becomes a story about a man who has always been furious discovering he is good at leading people who are worse. Supergirl joins for a crossover, which is exactly as odd as it sounds. The Lights Out and Godhead chapters are shared with the Venditti books.

VOLUME ven-o1 | Green Lantern by Robert Venditti Omnibus Vol. 1 | Robert Venditti, Van Jensen & Billy Tan | 2013–2014 | 36 issues
ABOUT Following Johns is impossible, and Robert Venditti does it by changing the job: Hal is no longer the hotshot, he is the leader of the Corps, and he is not good at it yet. The big idea is Relic, a scientist from the universe before this one, who discovers that the emotional spectrum is a finite reservoir and every ring is draining it — so the Lanterns are the threat. Lights Out turns the lights off. The Green Lantern Corps chapters, written by Van Jensen with Bernard Chang, run alongside as one story. Billy Tan's art is crisp, house-style superhero work.

VOLUME ven-o2 | Green Lantern by Robert Venditti Omnibus Vol. 2 | Robert Venditti & Billy Tan | 2014–2016 | 38 issues
ABOUT Godhead pits the whole spectrum against Kirby's New Gods, who want the white ring because they think it is the Life Equation, and it runs through every Lantern book at once. Then the run reinvents itself: Hal goes renegade with a stolen gauntlet, a beard and a truck, on the run from the Corps he used to lead — a Hal Jordan who is the outlaw for once instead of the cop. It closes the New 52 run at #52, and the next thing Venditti writes is the Rebirth relaunch that is not on this shelf.
